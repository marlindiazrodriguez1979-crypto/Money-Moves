👪 PARENT & GUARDIAN GUIDE
How to use this book as a family financial tool
Dear Parent or Guardian,

Money Moves was designed to give your teen a faith-based, practical foundation for managing money — the kind most adults wish they had at 14. The best outcomes happen when teens AND parents go through it together. This guide gives you the tools to make that happen.

💬 Weekly Conversation Starters
How to structure an allowance using 10-10-80 ▼

Give your teen their allowance in three envelopes or jars labeled Give, Save, and Spend. Even if the total is $10/week — $1 to give, $1 to save, $8 to spend — the habit forms at any amount. Celebrate every time they follow the system, not just the results.

Opening a teen bank account — what to look for ▼

Look for: no monthly fees, no minimum balance, a debit card with parental controls, and a linked savings account. Great options include Capital One MONEY (ages 8+), Chase First Banking, Greenlight, and Copper. Most require a parent/guardian as joint account holder. A High-Yield Savings Account (HYSA) through Marcus, Ally, or SoFi can earn 4–5% APY on the savings portion.

How to open a custodial investment account ▼

Custodial accounts (UGMA/UTMA) at Fidelity, Schwab, or Vanguard let a parent manage investments on behalf of a minor. The teen can watch and learn in real time. If your teen has earned income (babysitting, lawn work), a Custodial Roth IRA is the most powerful tool available — contributions grow tax-free for decades.

How to model good money habits at home ▼

Kids learn money by watching adults more than anything else. Normalize talking about money openly — explain your budget decisions, involve your teen in household financial discussions (at an age-appropriate level), let them see you giving, saving, and making intentional spending choices. The goal isn't perfection — it's transparency.

What to do if your teen makes a money mistake ▼

Money mistakes are learning moments, not failures. When your teen overspends or makes a poor financial choice: resist the urge to rescue them immediately, ask "What would you do differently?" before offering solutions, let natural consequences play out when safe to do so, and connect the mistake back to their stated goals. Shame closes the conversation; curiosity opens it.

FAMILY CHALLENGE
Do a "Family Money Meeting" this week. Sit together for 20 minutes and each person shares: one financial goal, one money win from this month, and one area to improve. No judgment — just honesty and encouragement.
